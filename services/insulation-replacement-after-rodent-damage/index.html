About this service

What Insulation Replacement Covers

Rodents nest in insulation and use it as a toilet. It absorbs urine and holds droppings, and tunnels and compression reduce its insulating value. Once contaminated, insulation is usually removed rather than cleaned.

Replacement comes after three steps: removing the animals, sealing the openings and cleaning the space. Putting new insulation over a problem that is still active only creates new nesting material. This page covers how removal is planned and what comes before replacement.

Signs

When to Call About Insulation Replacement

  • Flattened, stained or tunneled insulation
  • Droppings in the insulation
  • Urine smell
  • Visible nests
  • Higher heating or cooling bills with an attic problem

A common mistake

Blowing new insulation over soiled material is the usual error. It traps odor and invites new nesting. Remove first, then replace.

Research-based guidance

What Published Guidance Says About Insulation Replacement

Do not sweep or vacuum dry droppings

Dry insulation should never be vacuumed or swept. The CDC says not to sweep or vacuum dry droppings or nests, because that puts dust into the air. Wet them with disinfectant first and wipe them up. (Source: CDC, rodent cleanup)

Cleanup steps from the CDC

Removal follows cleanup steps. The CDC advises airing out the space for 30 minutes, wearing gloves, soaking droppings and nests with disinfectant, wiping them up with paper towels, double bagging the waste and washing hands afterward. (Source: CDC, rodent cleanup)

What works to seal gaps

Sealing comes before new insulation. UC IPM says gaps wider than a quarter inch should be closed with metal or concrete. Steel wool is a temporary plug, and it lists plastic, rubber, vinyl, expanding foam and wood as materials that do not hold against mice. (Source: UC IPM, house mouse)

Where rats nest

Nest findings decide how much insulation is removed. UC IPM explains that roof rats nest above ground, in attics and wall voids, while Norway rats burrow along foundations and under debris. The nesting place decides where traps, sealing and cleanup start. (Source: UC IPM, rats)

How small a gap a mouse needs

Mice need only a quarter inch, so entries are checked first. NC State Extension reports that an adult mouse can get through an opening of about a quarter inch. A gap that size is easy to miss at a door corner, a pipe collar or a vent edge. (Source: NC State Extension, common structure-invading mice)

Baiting a burrow

For Insulation Replacement: The EPA allows outdoor burrow baiting only with pelleted bait placed at least 6 inches down the burrow. Bait left on top of the ground is a hazard to pets and wildlife. Burrow work is for outdoor, controlled use only. (Source: US EPA, rodenticide product restrictions)

Checking traps daily

For Insulation Replacement: NC State Extension advises checking traps daily and keeping mouse traps for mice and rat traps for rats. A trap that is too small for the animal can injure it without killing it and teach it to avoid the spot. Daily checks also keep the work humane and the area clean. (Source: NC State Extension, common structure-invading mice)

Common questions

Insulation Replacement: Questions People Ask

Can rodent-soiled insulation be cleaned?

Generally no. It absorbs urine and droppings, so it is removed in bags, following CDC wet cleaning steps.

When should new insulation go in?

After the rodents are gone, entry points are sealed and the space is cleaned.

Is it dangerous to remove?

It can be if dust is stirred up. The CDC advises ventilating, wearing gloves and wetting before removal.

Will rodents damage new insulation?

Not if they cannot get in. UC IPM says gaps over a quarter inch should be closed with metal or concrete.
