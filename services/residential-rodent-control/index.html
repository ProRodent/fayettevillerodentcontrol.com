About this service

What Residential Rodent Control Covers

A house gives rodents many options: an attic under the roof, a crawl space below, a garage with a door that never quite seals and a yard with woodpiles, bird feeders and shrubs against the wall. A residential job looks at all of them together.

The visit starts with where you saw or heard activity, then works outward. Exterior entry points and attractants are listed, interior signs are mapped and a plan is built that covers trapping, sealing and cleanup. The goal is a house that stays closed to rodents after the work.

Signs

When to Call About Residential Rodent Control

  • Droppings in the kitchen or pantry
  • Noises in the attic or walls
  • Gnaw marks on food packaging
  • Burrows near the foundation
  • A musty smell in the garage

A common mistake

Treating only the room where you saw the rodent is the usual mistake. Rodents use the whole house, and entries are often elsewhere.

Research-based guidance

What Published Guidance Says About Residential Rodent Control

How small a gap a mouse needs

A house has dozens of quarter inch gaps. NC State Extension reports that an adult mouse can get through an opening of about a quarter inch. A gap that size is easy to miss at a door corner, a pipe collar or a vent edge. (Source: NC State Extension, common structure-invading mice)

Where rats nest

Where the nest is decides how the plan is built. UC IPM explains that roof rats nest above ground, in attics and wall voids, while Norway rats burrow along foundations and under debris. The nesting place decides where traps, sealing and cleanup start. (Source: UC IPM, rats)

Prevention is the main control

Sealing and sanitation come before bait or traps. The CDC states that controlling rodents in and around the home is the primary way to prevent rodent-borne disease such as hantavirus. Sealing and sanitation come before any trap or bait. (Source: CDC, rodent control)

Checking traps daily

Daily checks are part of a home job. NC State Extension advises checking traps daily and keeping mouse traps for mice and rat traps for rats. A trap that is too small for the animal can injure it without killing it and teach it to avoid the spot. (Source: NC State Extension, common structure-invading mice)

Where mouse traps go

Trap spacing is built from the guidance. UC IPM says mice seldom travel more than about 30 feet from the nest, so traps are spaced no more than about 10 feet apart and set in pairs 1 to 2 inches apart along walls where mice run. (Source: UC IPM, house mouse)

Door gaps and sweeps

For Residential Rodent Control: NC State Extension advises keeping the clearance under a door to no more than a quarter inch and mentions kick plates and rodent-proof door sweeps for garage and storage doors that take wear. A strip of daylight under a door is a gap that a rodent can use. (Source: NC State Extension, common structure-invading mice)

Common questions

Residential Rodent Control: Questions People Ask

Where do rodents usually enter a house?

Roofline gaps, vents, garage doors, foundation cracks and pipe or wire entries. NC State notes that mice use gaps about a quarter inch wide.

Is a single mouse a problem?

Often it is not alone. UC IPM reports that a female mouse can have 5 to 10 litters a year, so one can become many.

What should I do before the visit?

Clear access to the attic and crawl space, store food in sealed containers and note where you saw signs.

Will pets be at risk?

Indoor control uses traps. The EPA requires tamper-resistant stations wherever bait is used outdoors with pets nearby.
