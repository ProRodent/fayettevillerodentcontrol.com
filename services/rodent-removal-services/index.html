About this service

What Rodent Removal Covers

Rodent removal covers the live animals and the evidence they leave behind. For a rat that means trapping along its travel routes, and for a mouse it means paired traps and sealing. Either way, the visit also looks at what was drawing them in.

Removal is not the same as poison. Indoor work relies on traps, because the EPA notes that anticoagulant baits take days to kill and the animal can die in a wall. Bait, where it is used at all, goes in tamper-resistant stations outside.

Signs

When to Call About Rodent Removal

  • Sightings during the day, which can mean a large population
  • Fresh droppings in the same spot every day
  • Odor from a wall, ceiling or crawl space
  • Pet food or bird seed disturbed overnight
  • Gnaw marks on wiring or pipes

A common mistake

Using poison indoors is the mistake that leads to odor and flies when the animal dies in a wall. Another is removing animals but leaving food, water or shelter in place, which invites a new group.

Research-based guidance

What Published Guidance Says About Rodent Removal

Checking traps daily

Trap checks happen daily so animals are not left in place. NC State Extension advises checking traps daily and keeping mouse traps for mice and rat traps for rats. A trap that is too small for the animal can injure it without killing it and teach it to avoid the spot. (Source: NC State Extension, common structure-invading mice)

Why a dead rodent can end up in a wall

Poison is avoided indoors for a practical reason. The EPA says anticoagulant baits take about 4 days to 2 weeks to kill. A poisoned rodent can die inside a wall, attic or crawl space, which is why trapping is preferred indoors. (Source: US EPA, rodenticides)

Where rats nest

Where the animal nests decides where removal begins. UC IPM explains that roof rats nest above ground, in attics and wall voids, while Norway rats burrow along foundations and under debris. The nesting place decides where traps, sealing and cleanup start. (Source: UC IPM, rats)

How small a gap a mouse needs

Gap measurement matters even during removal. NC State Extension reports that an adult mouse can get through an opening of about a quarter inch. A gap that size is easy to miss at a door corner, a pipe collar or a vent edge. (Source: NC State Extension, common structure-invading mice)

Where rat traps go

Trap spacing is different for rats and mice. UC IPM says to space snap traps about 10 to 20 feet apart for rats and to set them off the ground, on beams or shelves, for roof rats, which travel along elevated routes. (Source: UC IPM, rats)

Common questions

Rodent Removal: Questions People Ask

Do you use poison to remove rodents?

Indoors the work uses traps. The EPA limits consumer rodenticides to ready-to-use bait stations and says poisoned rodents can die in walls, so bait is only used outside in tamper-resistant stations.

How long does removal take?

It depends on the species and how many animals there are. Mice can be reduced faster than a rat population in an attic, and trapping has to be checked every day until activity stops.

What if a rodent dies in the wall?

Odor and flies usually point to the spot. The carcass is located and removed, and the area is cleaned. See the dead rodent removal page for how that is handled.

Will rodents come back after removal?

They can if the entry points stay open. That is why removal is paired with sealing and with removing food and shelter.
