About this service

What Rodent Inspection Covers

A rodent inspection is the first step of every job. It answers four questions: what animal, where is it nesting, how is it getting in, and what is attracting it. The answers decide the treatment, the sealing work and the cleanup.

The walk covers the outside of the building, the roofline, the attic, the crawl space and the main living areas. For a business it also covers storage, dock doors, dumpsters and food areas. The result is a written scope of what needs to be done.

Signs

When to Call About Rodent Inspection

  • Any sign you cannot identify, such as unfamiliar droppings
  • Sounds you cannot place
  • A smell with no clear source
  • Gnawing in the attic or garage
  • A rodent seen once, which often means more

A common mistake

Guessing at the cause is the usual mistake. Treating the wrong species, or sealing the wrong area, wastes time and lets the problem grow. An inspection is how that guess gets replaced with facts.

What we look at

What Gets Checked During Rodent Inspection

Signs of activity

Droppings, gnawing, rub marks, burrows, nests and odors are recorded with their location.

Entry points

Gaps at doors, vents, pipes, wires and the roofline are measured against the sizes rodents can use.

Conditions that attract

Food, water, clutter, woodpiles and overgrown plantings near the building are noted.

Research-based guidance

What Published Guidance Says About Rodent Inspection

What mouse droppings tell you

Droppings are counted and sized to separate mice from rats. NC State Extension puts a mouse at roughly 50 to 75 droppings a day, each about a quarter to a half inch long. A small area can hold a lot of them, which is why a mouse problem is often found by droppings first. (Source: NC State Extension, common structure-invading mice)

Rub marks and travel routes

Rub marks reveal routes that droppings alone do not. NC State Extension lists rub marks along walls and pipes among the signs of rat travel. Those marks follow the same route night after night, which is where inspection and trapping effort pays off. (Source: NC State Extension, common structure-invading rats)

How small a gap a rat needs

Gap size is measured because it decides whether rats or only mice can pass. NC State Extension says young rats can pass through an opening of about a quarter inch and adult rats through about a half inch. That is smaller than most people expect when they look at a wall opening. (Source: NC State Extension, common structure-invading rats)

Where mouse traps go

Findings from the inspection decide trap placement. UC IPM says mice seldom travel more than about 30 feet from the nest, so traps are spaced no more than about 10 feet apart and set in pairs 1 to 2 inches apart along walls where mice run. (Source: UC IPM, house mouse)

Prevention is the main control

Inspection is where prevention starts. The CDC states that controlling rodents in and around the home is the primary way to prevent rodent-borne disease such as hantavirus. Sealing and sanitation come before any trap or bait. (Source: CDC, rodent control)
