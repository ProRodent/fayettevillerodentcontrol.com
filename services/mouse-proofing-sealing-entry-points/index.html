About this service

What Mouse Proofing Covers

Mice fit through openings of about a quarter inch, which is smaller than most people expect. They get in around garage doors, pipe and wire entries, dryer vents, foundation cracks and gaps where siding meets the slab.

Mouse proofing is a gap-by-gap job. Each opening is measured and closed with a material suited to it: steel mesh, metal flashing, concrete or a sweep. The goal is a building where mice have no easy way in, so traps are needed less often.

Signs

When to Call About Mouse Proofing

  • Droppings along baseboards or in drawers
  • Gaps around pipes under sinks
  • Light visible under a door
  • Gnawing at a garage door seal
  • Mice returning after each cleanup

A common mistake

Stuffing a gap with paper, plastic or foam is the most common mistake. Mice chew those materials. Steel wool is only a temporary plug, and metal mesh or concrete is what lasts.

What we look at

What Gets Checked During Mouse Proofing

Door and garage gaps

Door bottoms, corners and garage seals are checked, with sweeps or kick plates recommended where gaps exceed a quarter inch.

Pipes, wires and vents

Every place a pipe, wire or vent passes through a wall is checked and sealed with metal or mesh.

Foundation and siding

Cracks, mortar gaps and siding edges near the ground are checked for small openings.

Research-based guidance

What Published Guidance Says About Mouse Proofing

How small a gap a mouse needs

Quarter inch is the number that drives all mouse proofing. NC State Extension reports that an adult mouse can get through an opening of about a quarter inch. A gap that size is easy to miss at a door corner, a pipe collar or a vent edge. (Source: NC State Extension, common structure-invading mice)

Door gaps and sweeps

Doors are the most common mouse route in garages. NC State Extension advises keeping the clearance under a door to no more than a quarter inch and mentions kick plates and rodent-proof door sweeps for garage and storage doors that take wear. (Source: NC State Extension, common structure-invading mice)

What works to seal gaps

The materials list matters as much as the gap list. UC IPM says gaps wider than a quarter inch should be closed with metal or concrete. Steel wool is a temporary plug, and it lists plastic, rubber, vinyl, expanding foam and wood as materials that do not hold against mice. (Source: UC IPM, house mouse)

Where mouse traps go

Mouse proofing is paired with trap placement. UC IPM says mice seldom travel more than about 30 feet from the nest, so traps are spaced no more than about 10 feet apart and set in pairs 1 to 2 inches apart along walls where mice run. (Source: UC IPM, house mouse)

What mouse droppings tell you

Droppings show which gaps are in use. NC State Extension puts a mouse at roughly 50 to 75 droppings a day, each about a quarter to a half inch long. A small area can hold a lot of them, which is why a mouse problem is often found by droppings first. (Source: NC State Extension, common structure-invading mice)

Common questions

Mouse Proofing: Questions People Ask

Why do mice come back after trapping?

Usually because a gap is still open or food is still available. Trapping reduces the number of mice, and sealing keeps new ones from entering.

What does a good seal look like?

A tight fit of steel mesh, metal flashing or concrete. UC IPM says to use metal or concrete for gaps over a quarter inch and lists plastic, rubber, vinyl, foam and wood as materials that do not hold.

Can I seal gaps myself?

Small gaps can be plugged temporarily with steel wool. Larger or hard-to-reach openings are better handled with proper materials.

Do garage doors matter?

Yes. NC State advises keeping door clearance to a quarter inch or less and mentions kick plates and rodent-proof sweeps.
