About this service

What Rat Control Covers

Rat control is more than setting traps. The work starts with working out which rat you have, where it nests, where it travels and what lets it in, because each answer changes the plan. A Norway rat that burrows beside a foundation needs a different approach from a roof rat that lives in an attic.

In North Carolina the Norway rat is the most common structure-invading rat, according to NC State Extension, and roof rats turn up where trees, wires and roofs connect. A rat control visit in Fayetteville covers the outside of the building, the roofline, the attic and the crawl space, then ends with a written scope of what to trap, seal and clean.

Signs

When to Call About Rat Control

  • Scratching or running sounds in walls or the attic at night
  • Droppings larger than a grain of rice
  • Gnaw marks on wood, wires or food packaging
  • Greasy rub marks along walls and pipes
  • Burrow holes beside the foundation or under a shed

A common mistake

The most common mistake is trapping without sealing. Rats that lose their trapped companions simply fill the space again if the entry point is still open. A second mistake is setting a mouse trap for a rat, which can injure the animal and make it avoid the spot.

Research-based guidance

What Published Guidance Says About Rat Control

Telling the two rats apart

Telling Norway rats from roof rats comes first on a rat call. UC IPM gives roof rats at about 5 to 10 ounces and Norway rats at about 7 to 18 ounces. The species matters because roof rats work from above and Norway rats from the ground, so trap placement differs. (Source: UC IPM, rats)

What rat droppings tell you

Droppings are the quickest sign to confirm on a first look. NC State Extension puts a rat at roughly 30 to 50 droppings a day, each about a half to three quarters of an inch long. Fresh, dark droppings in a spot mean the route is in use now. (Source: NC State Extension, common structure-invading rats)

Where rat traps go

Trap spacing for rat control follows distances set by the guidance, not guesswork. UC IPM says to space snap traps about 10 to 20 feet apart for rats and to set them off the ground, on beams or shelves, for roof rats, which travel along elevated routes. (Source: UC IPM, rats)

How small a gap a rat needs

Sealing decisions for rat control start with this measurement. NC State Extension says young rats can pass through an opening of about a quarter inch and adult rats through about a half inch. That is smaller than most people expect when they look at a wall opening. (Source: NC State Extension, common structure-invading rats)

Prevention is the main control

The health reason behind rat control is well documented. The CDC states that controlling rodents in and around the home is the primary way to prevent rodent-borne disease such as hantavirus. Sealing and sanitation come before any trap or bait. (Source: CDC, rodent control)

Common questions

Rat Control: Questions People Ask

How do I know whether I have rats or mice?

Size and droppings are the main clues. NC State puts rat droppings at about a half to three quarters of an inch and mouse droppings at about a quarter to a half inch, so larger droppings and larger gnaw marks point to rats.

Where do rats nest in a house?

Roof rats nest above ground, often in attics and wall voids. Norway rats burrow along foundations and under debris, and may enter crawl spaces from there.

Will trapping alone solve a rat problem?

Not by itself. Rats return through any opening that stays open, so removal is paired with sealing entry points and removing food and shelter outside.

Is poison part of rat control?

Indoors the approach is trapping. The EPA limits consumer rodenticides to ready-to-use bait stations, and a poisoned rat can die inside a wall, so bait is used with care and only in tamper-resistant stations.
