About this service

What Mice Control Covers

House mice are small enough to use gaps most people never notice, and one female can produce many litters in a year. That is why a few droppings in a cabinet can turn into a problem in several rooms within weeks.

Mice control here means finding the activity, setting traps where mice actually run, and then closing the gaps they used. Without the sealing step the traps only slow the problem down. The visit covers kitchens, garages, utility rooms, attics and the outside of the building.

Signs

When to Call About Mice Control

  • Small droppings in drawers, cabinets or along baseboards
  • Shredded paper or fabric nests in quiet corners
  • Scratching sounds in walls at night
  • Chewed food packaging in the pantry
  • A musty or ammonia-like smell in closed spaces

A common mistake

People often put out one or two traps and stop when the first catch is made. Mice breed quickly, so more are usually behind it. Another common mistake is plugging a hole with foam or plastic, which mice chew through within days.

Research-based guidance

What Published Guidance Says About Mice Control

How small a gap a mouse needs

Gap size is the first thing measured on a mouse call. NC State Extension reports that an adult mouse can get through an opening of about a quarter inch. A gap that size is easy to miss at a door corner, a pipe collar or a vent edge. (Source: NC State Extension, common structure-invading mice)

How fast mice multiply

Speed of breeding is why mouse problems are treated quickly. UC IPM reports that a female house mouse can have 5 to 10 litters a year of about 5 to 6 pups, with gestation of 18 to 21 days and sexual maturity at 5 to 6 weeks. A few mice can become dozens in a season. (Source: UC IPM, house mouse)

Where mouse traps go

Trap spacing is set by how far mice actually travel. UC IPM says mice seldom travel more than about 30 feet from the nest, so traps are spaced no more than about 10 feet apart and set in pairs 1 to 2 inches apart along walls where mice run. (Source: UC IPM, house mouse)

Checking traps daily

Daily trap checks are part of every mouse job. NC State Extension advises checking traps daily and keeping mouse traps for mice and rat traps for rats. A trap that is too small for the animal can injure it without killing it and teach it to avoid the spot. (Source: NC State Extension, common structure-invading mice)

Bait is for stations only

Loose bait is not a safe answer indoors. UC IPM notes that residential rodenticide use is limited to ready-to-use bait stations. Loose bait in an attic or crawl space is not a home-use option, and it is a risk to pets and children. (Source: UC IPM, house mouse)

Common questions

Mice Control: Questions People Ask

How small a gap can a mouse use?

NC State Extension reports that an adult mouse can get through an opening of about a quarter inch. That includes door corners, pipe collars and vent edges.

Why do mice keep coming back?

If a gap stays open or food stays available, new mice move in. Sealing with metal or mesh and storing food in sealed containers is what stops the cycle.

Where should mouse traps go?

Along walls, in corners and behind appliances where mice run, in pairs, about 10 feet apart. UC IPM notes that mice seldom travel more than about 30 feet.

Is expanding foam enough to seal a gap?

No. UC IPM lists expanding foam, plastic, rubber, vinyl and wood as materials that do not hold against mice. Metal or concrete is what works, with steel wool as a temporary plug.
