About this service

What Rodent Exclusion Covers

Exclusion means finding every opening a rodent can use and closing it with a material it cannot chew through. It covers the roofline, soffits and vents, the foundation, utility penetrations, garage doors and crawl space access.

The reason it matters is simple. Removing the rodents inside a building does nothing if the same gap stays open. Exclusion is done after or alongside trapping so animals are not sealed in, and it uses metal, mesh and concrete rather than foam or plastic.

Signs

When to Call About Rodent Exclusion

  • Droppings or gnawing near a vent, door or pipe
  • Daylight visible around a door or pipe
  • Grease marks around a hole in siding or foundation
  • A new rodent problem after trapping stopped
  • Noises that return a few weeks after removal

A common mistake

The usual mistake is sealing too soon, which traps animals inside walls. The other is using weak material. Rodents chew through foam, plastic and wood, so the seal fails and the work is repeated.

What we look at

What Gets Checked During Rodent Exclusion

Whole building survey

Roof edges, vents, foundation, doors and every wire and pipe that enters the building are checked and marked.

Material choice

Hardware cloth, metal flashing, concrete and steel mesh are used where rodents chew; foam is not used as a stand-alone seal.

Order of work

Sealing is timed so animals are out first, with one-way routes or trapping used where needed before the last gaps are closed.

Research-based guidance

What Published Guidance Says About Rodent Exclusion

Vents need metal, not filler

Vents are the most common large opening, so they get metal mesh. NC State Extension recommends quarter inch hardware cloth over vents and does not recommend expanding foam as a stand-alone seal. Rodents chew soft materials, so metal mesh is what keeps an opening closed. (Source: NC State Extension, common structure-invading rats)

What works to seal gaps

The right material matters more than the amount of material. UC IPM says gaps wider than a quarter inch should be closed with metal or concrete. Steel wool is a temporary plug, and it lists plastic, rubber, vinyl, expanding foam and wood as materials that do not hold against mice. (Source: UC IPM, house mouse)

Door gaps and sweeps

Garage and entry doors wear out first and need a closer look. NC State Extension advises keeping the clearance under a door to no more than a quarter inch and mentions kick plates and rodent-proof door sweeps for garage and storage doors that take wear. (Source: NC State Extension, common structure-invading mice)

How fast mice multiply

Fast breeding is why gaps cannot be left for later. UC IPM reports that a female house mouse can have 5 to 10 litters a year of about 5 to 6 pups, with gestation of 18 to 21 days and sexual maturity at 5 to 6 weeks. A few mice can become dozens in a season. (Source: UC IPM, house mouse)

Where rats nest

Nest location tells you which openings matter most. UC IPM explains that roof rats nest above ground, in attics and wall voids, while Norway rats burrow along foundations and under debris. The nesting place decides where traps, sealing and cleanup start. (Source: UC IPM, rats)

Common questions

Rodent Exclusion: Questions People Ask

What is rodent exclusion?

It is sealing the openings that let rodents enter a building, using materials they cannot chew through, such as hardware cloth, metal flashing and concrete.

How small an opening needs to be sealed?

Anything about a quarter inch or larger. NC State notes that young rats and adult mice use quarter inch gaps and adult rats use about a half inch.

Can rodents chew through foam?

Yes. NC State does not recommend expanding foam alone, and UC IPM lists it among materials that do not hold against mice.

Do you seal before or after trapping?

Both are planned together. Sealing is timed so rodents are not trapped inside walls, and the last gaps are closed once activity has stopped.
