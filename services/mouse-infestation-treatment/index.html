About this service

What Mouse Infestation Treatment Covers

An infestation means mice have settled in: nests, droppings in several rooms and activity at night. By then there are usually multiple animals. UC IPM reports that a female house mouse can have 5 to 10 litters a year, so a small group can grow quickly.

Treatment combines three things. Trapping, in paired sets along runways. Sealing, so new mice cannot enter. Sanitation, so food and nesting material are not available. Treating only one of these usually brings the problem back.

Signs

When to Call About Mouse Infestation Treatment

  • Droppings in several rooms
  • Nests in drawers, boxes or walls
  • Chewed food and packaging
  • Noises at night
  • A strong ammonia smell

A common mistake

Treating one room is the most common mistake. An established infestation usually spans several rooms. Another is skipping sealing, so new mice replace the trapped ones.

What we look at

What Gets Checked During Mouse Infestation Treatment

Trapping grid

Paired traps are set about 10 feet apart along walls and runways and checked daily until catches stop.

Sealing

Quarter inch gaps at doors, pipes, vents and foundations are closed with metal, mesh or concrete.

Sanitation

Food is stored in sealed containers, crumbs and pet food are removed and droppings are cleaned safely.

Research-based guidance

What Published Guidance Says About Mouse Infestation Treatment

How fast mice multiply

Breeding speed explains why partial treatment fails. UC IPM reports that a female house mouse can have 5 to 10 litters a year of about 5 to 6 pups, with gestation of 18 to 21 days and sexual maturity at 5 to 6 weeks. A few mice can become dozens in a season. (Source: UC IPM, house mouse)

Where mouse traps go

A trap grid has to cover the whole run, not one corner. UC IPM says mice seldom travel more than about 30 feet from the nest, so traps are spaced no more than about 10 feet apart and set in pairs 1 to 2 inches apart along walls where mice run. (Source: UC IPM, house mouse)

Bait is for stations only

Bait is not a household option except in stations. UC IPM notes that residential rodenticide use is limited to ready-to-use bait stations. Loose bait in an attic or crawl space is not a home-use option, and it is a risk to pets and children. (Source: UC IPM, house mouse)

How small a gap a mouse needs

Mice need only a quarter inch. NC State Extension reports that an adult mouse can get through an opening of about a quarter inch. A gap that size is easy to miss at a door corner, a pipe collar or a vent edge. (Source: NC State Extension, common structure-invading mice)

Cleanup steps from the CDC

Cleanup follows safe steps after treatment. The CDC advises airing out the space for 30 minutes, wearing gloves, soaking droppings and nests with disinfectant, wiping them up with paper towels, double bagging the waste and washing hands afterward. (Source: CDC, rodent cleanup)

Telling the two rats apart

For Mouse Infestation Treatment: UC IPM gives roof rats at about 5 to 10 ounces and Norway rats at about 7 to 18 ounces. The species matters because roof rats work from above and Norway rats from the ground, so trap placement differs. Size is one more clue to add to droppings and nest location. (Source: UC IPM, rats)

How long does a mouse infestation take to clear?

It depends on the size of the population and whether the entry points are closed. Trapping continues, with daily checks, until catches stop.

Why use paired traps?

UC IPM advises setting traps in pairs 1 to 2 inches apart along walls, because mice run along edges.

Can I use bait?

UC IPM notes that residential rodenticide use is limited to ready-to-use bait stations. Indoors, traps are preferred.

How do I clean up after?

The CDC advises airing out the space, wearing gloves, wetting droppings with disinfectant and wiping them up rather than sweeping.
