Restaurant Rodent Control on Hay Street: What Downtown Fayetteville Businesses Need to Know

Published March 18, 2026

Restaurants give rodents food, water and warmth. This guide covers where to look, how to place traps and what to ask for when you call, using published guidance.

Key Takeaways

  • Start with back doors, dry storage and the dumpster area
  • Use the right trap for the animal and check traps daily
  • Written notes of findings help managers, but no one can promise an inspection result

Why Restaurants Are Targets

A kitchen offers food, water, shelter and warm equipment, and doors that open all day. Downtown Fayetteville has a long history. The city of Fayetteville traces back to Cross Creek and Campbelton, which merged in 1783, and the Market House was completed in 1832. Many downtown buildings are old, and older buildings tend to have more gaps at foundations, pipes and doors. That is a general pattern. We found no published source ranking downtown restaurants by rodent pressure, and we do not claim one.

Back Doors and Receiving

Door gaps and sweeps

The back door is where this guidance applies most. NC State Extension advises keeping the clearance under a door to no more than a quarter inch and mentions kick plates and rodent-proof door sweeps for garage and storage doors that take wear. (Source: NC State Extension, common structure-invading mice)

How small a gap a rat needs

Rats need about a half inch, mice about a quarter. NC State Extension says young rats can pass through an opening of about a quarter inch and adult rats through about a half inch. That is smaller than most people expect when they look at a wall opening. (Source: NC State Extension, common structure-invading rats)

Check thresholds, door corners and the point where the door meets the floor. Propped doors are the largest gap of all. Put a sweep or kick plate on any door that takes traffic.

Dry Storage and Kitchen

What mouse droppings tell you

Droppings along shelf edges and behind equipment are often the first sign. NC State Extension puts a mouse at roughly 50 to 75 droppings a day, each about a quarter to a half inch long. A small area can hold a lot of them, which is why a mouse problem is often found by droppings first. (Source: NC State Extension, common structure-invading mice)

Rub marks and travel routes

Rub marks behind equipment show runways. NC State Extension lists rub marks along walls and pipes among the signs of rat travel. Those marks follow the same route night after night, which is where inspection and trapping effort pays off. (Source: NC State Extension, common structure-invading rats)

Keep food off the floor, in sealed containers, and keep boxes away from walls so the base of the wall is visible.

Dumpsters and Drains

Outside, rats feed on food waste. Dumpster lids that stay closed, pads that are cleaned and drains that are covered reduce what is available. Floor drains are checked for gaps where pipes enter.

Where rats nest

Norway rats burrow under debris, so stacked pallets and boxes near the dumpster get moved. UC IPM explains that roof rats nest above ground, in attics and wall voids, while Norway rats burrow along foundations and under debris. The nesting place decides where traps, sealing and cleanup start. (Source: UC IPM, rats)

Traps, Not Poison, Inside

Checking traps daily

This applies in a kitchen as much as in a house. NC State Extension advises checking traps daily and keeping mouse traps for mice and rat traps for rats. A trap that is too small for the animal can injure it without killing it and teach it to avoid the spot. (Source: NC State Extension, common structure-invading mice)

Why a dead rodent can end up in a wall

This is why poison is a poor choice inside a food business. The EPA says anticoagulant baits take about 4 days to 2 weeks to kill. A poisoned rodent can die inside a wall, attic or crawl space, which is why trapping is preferred indoors. (Source: US EPA, rodenticides)

Where mouse traps go

Spacing is set by the animal, not by the room. UC IPM says mice seldom travel more than about 30 feet from the nest, so traps are spaced no more than about 10 feet apart and set in pairs 1 to 2 inches apart along walls where mice run. (Source: UC IPM, house mouse)

More From the Published Guidance

Where rat traps go

UC IPM says to space snap traps about 10 to 20 feet apart for rats and to set them off the ground, on beams or shelves, for roof rats, which travel along elevated routes. Spacing and height both change how often a trap is found by the animal. (Source: UC IPM, rats)

What rat droppings tell you

Fresh, dark droppings in a spot mean the route is in use now. In practice, that makes droppings the first thing to photograph and count. (Source: NC State Extension, common structure-invading rats)

Telling the two rats apart

UC IPM gives roof rats at about 5 to 10 ounces and Norway rats at about 7 to 18 ounces. The species matters because roof rats work from above and Norway rats from the ground, so trap placement differs. Size is one more clue to add to droppings and nest location. (Source: UC IPM, rats)

Storms and flooding push rodents indoors

NC State Extension notes that flooding pushes rodents into attics and that Norway rats are the most common rat in North Carolina. After a storm, new gaps and wet debris are both worth checking. A quick walk around the property after a storm is time well spent. (Source: NC State Extension, pest problems after storms: rodents)

Vents need metal, not filler

NC State Extension recommends quarter inch hardware cloth over vents and does not recommend expanding foam as a stand-alone seal. Rodents chew soft materials, so metal mesh is what keeps an opening closed. Replacing a torn screen is one of the cheapest steps with the biggest effect. (Source: NC State Extension, common structure-invading rats)

How fast mice multiply

UC IPM reports that a female house mouse can have 5 to 10 litters a year of about 5 to 6 pups, with gestation of 18 to 21 days and sexual maturity at 5 to 6 weeks. A few mice can become dozens in a season. Waiting a few weeks can multiply the number of animals to deal with. (Source: UC IPM, house mouse)
