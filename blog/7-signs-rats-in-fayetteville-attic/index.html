7 Signs you have Rats in Your Fayetteville Attic (And What to Do Next)

Published December 2, 2025

Attic rat activity is usually heard before it is seen. Here are seven signs that point to rats, what each one means and what to do when you confirm a problem.

Key Takeaways

  • Scratching at night, droppings, gnaw marks, rub marks, disturbed insulation, odor and roofline gaps are the seven signs
  • UC IPM says roof rats nest above ground in attics and wall voids, so the attic is where roof rats are found
  • Signs are confirmed with an inspection before any trapping or sealing starts

Sign 1: Scratching and Running Overhead

Sound is usually the first sign. Rats are active mostly at night, so scratching, running or thumping in the ceiling after dark is the classic report. Animals that are active in daylight, such as squirrels, tend to make noise in the morning and afternoon, while rat noise picks up after dusk. Pipes and ductwork also tick as they heat and cool, but that sound is regular and mechanical, not irregular and animal.

Where rats nest

Roof rats are the rats most likely to be in an attic, and that is why overhead noise is taken seriously. UC IPM explains that roof rats nest above ground, in attics and wall voids, while Norway rats burrow along foundations and under debris. The nesting place decides where traps, sealing and cleanup start. (Source: UC IPM, rats)

Sign 2: Droppings in the Insulation

Droppings are the clearest confirmation. Look along joists, near vents and around insulation that looks disturbed. Fresh droppings are dark and moist looking. Older ones are gray, dry and crumbly. A mix of both means the animals are still using the space.

What rat droppings tell you

A few droppings can mean a visit, but a lot means a nest. NC State Extension puts a rat at roughly 30 to 50 droppings a day, each about a half to three quarters of an inch long. Fresh, dark droppings in a spot mean the route is in use now. (Source: NC State Extension, common structure-invading rats)

Do not sweep or vacuum dry droppings. The cleanup section below covers the safe method.

Sign 3: Gnaw Marks on Wood, Wiring and Ducts

Rats gnaw to keep their teeth worn down. In an attic, that means bright, fresh scratches on rafters and joists, chewed insulation on wires, gnawed ductwork and shredded cardboard. Gnawed wiring is the finding that matters most, because damaged insulation on a wire is a repair that should not wait. Fresh marks are pale. Older marks darken with dust and age.

Gnaw marks help locate the route. Follow them to find the vent, soffit or pipe gap the animal uses, and to see where traps will work best.

Sign 4: Rub Marks Along Joists and Beams

Rub marks and travel routes

Dark, greasy smears along joists and beams tell you which way the rats travel. NC State Extension lists rub marks along walls and pipes among the signs of rat travel. Those marks follow the same route night after night, which is where inspection and trapping effort pays off. (Source: NC State Extension, common structure-invading rats)

Fresh rub marks look slightly glossy. Old ones are dry and dark. Several marks along one beam mean the route has been used many times, which points to an established animal instead of a one-time visitor.

Sign 5: Disturbed or Flattened Insulation

Rats nest in insulation. A flattened patch, a small tunnel through it or a pile of shredded material is a nest. Nests are usually in a protected spot away from the entry point and close to where the animals travel. If you find one, avoid disturbing it. Take a photo from a distance and call.

Telling the two rats apart

The size of the animal helps confirm the species that built the nest. UC IPM gives roof rats at about 5 to 10 ounces and Norway rats at about 7 to 18 ounces. The species matters because roof rats work from above and Norway rats from the ground, so trap placement differs. (Source: UC IPM, rats)

Sign 6: A Musty or Ammonia Smell

A heavy rat population leaves an odor from urine, often noticed first in upper floor rooms or near the attic hatch. A strong smell with no plumbing or moisture source is a reason to inspect. A strong smell that grows in one spot can also mean a dead animal. See our dead rodent removal page if that is what you smell.

Sign 7: Gaps at the Roofline

The last sign is outside. From the ground or a ladder, look at the eaves, soffits, gable vents and where wires or pipes enter. Dark staining around an opening, bent screens and gaps where wood has pulled away are entry points. UC IPM notes that roof rats reach roofs along tree limbs and wires, so limbs touching the house matter as much as the opening itself.

Cutting the route to the roof

Check for limbs and wires that touch the roof. UC IPM lists limb trimming, with a clearance range of 3 to 6 feet, as a way to cut off the route roof rats use to reach a roof. Trees and wires that touch the house act as bridges. (Source: UC IPM, rats)

Screening vents against rats

Closing a vent with the wrong material only delays the next entry. UC IPM recommends half inch mesh over vents and says chicken wire is not enough to stop rats. NC State's quarter inch hardware cloth is the tighter standard, and it also keeps mice out. (Source: UC IPM, rats)

What to Do Once You Confirm It

Call before setting traps or sealing anything. Sealing before the animals are out traps them in the structure, and trapping without a plan can miss the route. The inspection confirms the species, finds the nest and lists the openings. Trapping, sealing and cleanup follow in that order.

Cleanup steps from the CDC

Cleanup comes last, once activity has stopped. The CDC advises airing out the space for 30 minutes, wearing gloves, soaking droppings and nests with disinfectant, wiping them up with paper towels, double bagging the waste and washing hands afterward. (Source: CDC, rodent cleanup)

More From the Published Guidance

Baiting a burrow

The EPA allows outdoor burrow baiting only with pelleted bait placed at least 6 inches down the burrow. Bait left on top of the ground is a hazard to pets and wildlife. Burrow work is for outdoor, controlled use only. (Source: US EPA, rodenticide product restrictions)

Storm debris is shelter

NC State Extension says debris left by a storm gives rodents shelter near a home. Stacked limbs, damaged fencing and soaked stored items near the foundation should be moved or removed. Removing debris takes away shelter before animals use it. (Source: NC State Extension, pest problems after storms: rodents)

Tamper-resistant stations

The EPA requires tamper-resistant bait stations where children under six, pets or wildlife could reach the bait. A station that a child or a dog can open is not an acceptable placement. Placement matters as much as the product. (Source: US EPA, rodenticide product restrictions)

What mouse droppings tell you

NC State Extension puts a mouse at roughly 50 to 75 droppings a day, each about a quarter to a half inch long. A small area can hold a lot of them, which is why a mouse problem is often found by droppings first. That volume is why even a small mouse problem leaves obvious evidence. (Source: NC State Extension, common structure-invading mice)
