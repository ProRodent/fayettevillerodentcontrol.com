Cape Fear River Corridor and Norway Rat Pressure: What Fayetteville Property Owners Should Know

Published January 8, 2026

Norway rats burrow along foundations, banks and debris, and Fayetteville sits on the Cape Fear River and Cross Creek. Here is what the published sources say and what property owners near water can check.

Key Takeaways

  • UC IPM says Norway rats burrow along foundations and beneath rubbish and woodpiles
  • NC State notes that flooding and wind push rodents into homes, often into attics
  • Properties near creeks and rivers should check the foundation edge first

The River and the City

The Cape Fear River runs about 191 miles and flows southeast past Lillington, Fayetteville and Elizabethtown, according to Wikipedia. Fayetteville itself had 208,501 residents at the 2020 census, and Cross Creek flows through the city to the Cape Fear River. Water edges are common in the area, and so are the places where people and rodents meet: banks, culverts, drainage ditches and foundation edges.

These are facts about the landscape. They are not a measurement of how many rats live in any neighborhood, and no published source we found ranks Fayetteville neighborhoods by rat pressure. What can be said is what rats do near structures, and where a property owner should look.

What Norway Rats Do Near Structures

Where rats nest

Water edges, foundations and debris piles are where burrows start. UC IPM explains that roof rats nest above ground, in attics and wall voids, while Norway rats burrow along foundations and under debris. The nesting place decides where traps, sealing and cleanup start. (Source: UC IPM, rats)

Telling the two rats apart

Norway rats are the heavier of the two common rats, and they stay at ground level. UC IPM gives roof rats at about 5 to 10 ounces and Norway rats at about 7 to 18 ounces. The species matters because roof rats work from above and Norway rats from the ground, so trap placement differs. (Source: UC IPM, rats)

A burrow looks like a hole in soft soil along a foundation, under a slab edge, beside a shed or under a woodpile. Fresh burrows have clean, loose soil at the entrance. Worn paths in grass or along fences, called runways, connect burrows to food.

Flooding and Storms

Storms and flooding push rodents indoors

The Cape Fear and its tributaries can rise after heavy rain and storms, which is when this matters. NC State Extension notes that flooding pushes rodents into attics and that Norway rats are the most common rat in North Carolina. After a storm, new gaps and wet debris are both worth checking. (Source: NC State Extension, pest problems after storms: rodents)

Storm debris is shelter

After a storm, walk the yard for debris stacks that rodents could use. NC State Extension says debris left by a storm gives rodents shelter near a home. Stacked limbs, damaged fencing and soaked stored items near the foundation should be moved or removed. (Source: NC State Extension, pest problems after storms: rodents)

After high water, check crawl space vents for damage, look for new burrows along the foundation and check the attic for noises. Displaced animals look for dry ground and shelter.

What to Check on a Property Near Water

Start at the foundation. Look for burrows, gnaw marks at the base of walls and soil that has washed away around vents. Then check the garage door seal, pipe entries and crawl space access. Outside, move woodpiles away from the house, trim vegetation back from the foundation and keep trash and pet food in sealed containers.

How small a gap a rat needs

Any gap this size at ground level is a route in. NC State Extension says young rats can pass through an opening of about a quarter inch and adult rats through about a half inch. That is smaller than most people expect when they look at a wall opening. (Source: NC State Extension, common structure-invading rats)

Vents need metal, not filler

Crawl space vents are the most common weak point near the ground. NC State Extension recommends quarter inch hardware cloth over vents and does not recommend expanding foam as a stand-alone seal. Rodents chew soft materials, so metal mesh is what keeps an opening closed. (Source: NC State Extension, common structure-invading rats)

Tools That Fit the Setting

Baiting a burrow

If bait is used near a burrow, the EPA sets the rule. The EPA allows outdoor burrow baiting only with pelleted bait placed at least 6 inches down the burrow. Bait left on top of the ground is a hazard to pets and wildlife. (Source: US EPA, rodenticide product restrictions)

Tamper-resistant stations

Where stations are used, they must be tamper resistant. The EPA requires tamper-resistant bait stations where children under six, pets or wildlife could reach the bait. A station that a child or a dog can open is not an acceptable placement. (Source: US EPA, rodenticide product restrictions)

Sealing and sanitation do most of the work. Traps and stations handle the animals already present.

More From the Published Guidance

Where mouse traps go

UC IPM says mice seldom travel more than about 30 feet from the nest, so traps are spaced no more than about 10 feet apart and set in pairs 1 to 2 inches apart along walls where mice run. Placing traps by the numbers works better than scattering them. (Source: UC IPM, house mouse)

What works to seal gaps

UC IPM says gaps wider than a quarter inch should be closed with metal or concrete. Steel wool is a temporary plug, and it lists plastic, rubber, vinyl, expanding foam and wood as materials that do not hold against mice. Choosing the right material the first time avoids repeat visits. (Source: UC IPM, house mouse)

Where rat traps go

UC IPM says to space snap traps about 10 to 20 feet apart for rats and to set them off the ground, on beams or shelves, for roof rats, which travel along elevated routes. Spacing and height both change how often a trap is found by the animal. (Source: UC IPM, rats)

What rat droppings tell you

NC State Extension puts a rat at roughly 30 to 50 droppings a day, each about a half to three quarters of an inch long. Fresh, dark droppings in a spot mean the route is in use now. In practice, that makes droppings the first thing to photograph and count. (Source: NC State Extension, common structure-invading rats)

Checking traps daily

NC State Extension advises checking traps daily and keeping mouse traps for mice and rat traps for rats. A trap that is too small for the animal can injure it without killing it and teach it to avoid the spot. Daily checks also keep the work humane and the area clean. (Source: NC State Extension, common structure-invading mice)

Cutting the route to the roof

UC IPM lists limb trimming, with a clearance range of 3 to 6 feet, as a way to cut off the route roof rats use to reach a roof. Trees and wires that touch the house act as bridges. Clearance is a one-time job that keeps a route closed for years. (Source: UC IPM, rats)
