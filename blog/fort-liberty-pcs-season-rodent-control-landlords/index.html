Fort Bragg PCS Moves and Rodent Control: A Guide for Landlords

Published February 19, 2026

Between tenants is the easiest time to inspect a rental for rodents. This guide gives landlords near Fort Bragg a checklist for move-out and move-in, built on published guidance.

Key Takeaways

  • Inspect at move-out, while any signs are still easy to see
  • Close gaps of a quarter inch or more with metal or concrete
  • Check again before the next tenant moves in

Why Turnover Is the Right Time

Fort Bragg is a large installation next to Fayetteville. Wikipedia lists more than 52,000 military personnel there and gives the post an area of about 251 square miles. The post was Camp Bragg in 1918 and Fort Bragg from September 30, 1922. It was renamed Fort Liberty in 2023 and went back to Fort Bragg effective February 14, 2025. Rentals near the post turn over as families move, and each turnover is a chance to inspect an empty house.

We found no published figure for how many rentals near the post have rodents, and no source that ties rodent activity to the moving calendar, so this guide does not claim either. What we can say is that a vacant house is easier to inspect than an occupied one, and that problems found before a new tenant arrives are easier to fix.

Move-Out Inspection: What to Check

How small a gap a mouse needs

Start with gaps, since mice need only a quarter inch. NC State Extension reports that an adult mouse can get through an opening of about a quarter inch. A gap that size is easy to miss at a door corner, a pipe collar or a vent edge. (Source: NC State Extension, common structure-invading mice)

Door gaps and sweeps

Garage and exterior doors take the most wear. NC State Extension advises keeping the clearance under a door to no more than a quarter inch and mentions kick plates and rodent-proof door sweeps for garage and storage doors that take wear. (Source: NC State Extension, common structure-invading mice)

Walk the inside: kitchen cabinets and the space behind the stove and fridge, the water heater closet, the garage, the attic hatch and the crawl space access. Look for droppings, gnawing, nesting material and odors. Outside, check the foundation edge for burrows, the vents for torn screens and the roofline for gaps.

Reading What You Find

What mouse droppings tell you

Counts of droppings help judge how active the problem is. NC State Extension puts a mouse at roughly 50 to 75 droppings a day, each about a quarter to a half inch long. A small area can hold a lot of them, which is why a mouse problem is often found by droppings first. (Source: NC State Extension, common structure-invading mice)

What rat droppings tell you

Larger droppings suggest rats, which means a bigger entry point. NC State Extension puts a rat at roughly 30 to 50 droppings a day, each about a half to three quarters of an inch long. Fresh, dark droppings in a spot mean the route is in use now. (Source: NC State Extension, common structure-invading rats)

If you find signs, record where and take photos. A written note with locations helps the next step, whether that is trapping, sealing or cleanup.

Fixing Problems Before the Next Tenant

What works to seal gaps

Use metal and concrete for gaps, not foam or plastic. UC IPM says gaps wider than a quarter inch should be closed with metal or concrete. Steel wool is a temporary plug, and it lists plastic, rubber, vinyl, expanding foam and wood as materials that do not hold against mice. (Source: UC IPM, house mouse)

Checking traps daily

If traps are used, they have to be checked daily, so schedule the work when someone can do that. NC State Extension advises checking traps daily and keeping mouse traps for mice and rat traps for rats. A trap that is too small for the animal can injure it without killing it and teach it to avoid the spot. (Source: NC State Extension, common structure-invading mice)

Cleanup steps from the CDC

Cleanup before a new tenant moves in follows the CDC steps. The CDC advises airing out the space for 30 minutes, wearing gloves, soaking droppings and nests with disinfectant, wiping them up with paper towels, double bagging the waste and washing hands afterward. (Source: CDC, rodent cleanup)

Before the Tenant Moves In

Do a short second check once the house is ready: doors close tight, vents are screened, nothing is chewed and no droppings are present. A written summary gives the new tenant a clean starting point and gives you a record.

Tell new tenants what helps: keep food in sealed containers, keep trash bagged and tied, and report signs early. Early reports are cheaper to fix than late ones.

More From the Published Guidance

Vents need metal, not filler

NC State Extension recommends quarter inch hardware cloth over vents and does not recommend expanding foam as a stand-alone seal. Rodents chew soft materials, so metal mesh is what keeps an opening closed. Replacing a torn screen is one of the cheapest steps with the biggest effect. (Source: NC State Extension, common structure-invading rats)

Bait is for stations only

UC IPM notes that residential rodenticide use is limited to ready-to-use bait stations. Loose bait in an attic or crawl space is not a home-use option, and it is a risk to pets and children. That limit is why loose bait is never part of a home plan. (Source: UC IPM, house mouse)

Cutting the route to the roof

For Fort Bragg PCS Moves and Rodent Control: A Guide for Landlords: UC IPM lists limb trimming, with a clearance range of 3 to 6 feet, as a way to cut off the route roof rats use to reach a roof. Trees and wires that touch the house act as bridges. Clearance is a one-time job that keeps a route closed for years. (Source: UC IPM, rats)

Baiting a burrow

The EPA allows outdoor burrow baiting only with pelleted bait placed at least 6 inches down the burrow. Bait left on top of the ground is a hazard to pets and wildlife. Burrow work is for outdoor, controlled use only. (Source: US EPA, rodenticide product restrictions)

Where mouse traps go

For Fort Bragg PCS Moves and Rodent Control: A Guide for Landlords: UC IPM says mice seldom travel more than about 30 feet from the nest, so traps are spaced no more than about 10 feet apart and set in pairs 1 to 2 inches apart along walls where mice run. Placing traps by the numbers works better than scattering them. (Source: UC IPM, house mouse)

Prevention is the main control

The CDC states that controlling rodents in and around the home is the primary way to prevent rodent-borne disease such as hantavirus. Sealing and sanitation come before any trap or bait. Prevention work, not cleanup alone, is the main control. (Source: CDC, rodent control)
