Crawl Space Rodent Sealing in the Sandhills: Vents, Edges and Access

Published February 5, 2026

Crawl space vents, access doors and foundation edges are where rats and mice reach the space under a house. This guide covers what to check and which materials hold.

Key Takeaways

  • NC State recommends quarter inch hardware cloth on vents and says foam alone will not hold
  • Norway rats burrow along foundations, so soil around vents is part of the check
  • Seal after trapping so animals are not shut in

The Sandhills Setting

The Sandhills is a 10 to 35 mile wide region along the inland edge of the Atlantic Coastal Plain, with very sandy soils (Alpin and Candor sand are the main mapped types) and pine-dominated vegetation, according to Wikipedia. Fayetteville sits near the edge of that region. Loose, sandy ground can wash or shift around foundation edges, which makes it worth checking the soil around vents and footings.

This guide does not claim that sandy soil makes rats burrow faster or more often. We found no published source for that. What can be said is how rodents behave near foundations in general, which is covered below.

How Rodents Reach a Crawl Space

Where rats nest

Norway rats start at the foundation edge, so that is where to look first. UC IPM explains that roof rats nest above ground, in attics and wall voids, while Norway rats burrow along foundations and under debris. The nesting place decides where traps, sealing and cleanup start. (Source: UC IPM, rats)

How small a gap a mouse needs

Mice use gaps much smaller than a rat needs. NC State Extension reports that an adult mouse can get through an opening of about a quarter inch. A gap that size is easy to miss at a door corner, a pipe collar or a vent edge. (Source: NC State Extension, common structure-invading mice)

Common routes are torn or missing vent screens, gaps around the access door, openings where pipes or wires enter the foundation and cracks in the masonry. Each is checked from the outside and again from inside the crawl space.

Screening Vents

Vents need metal, not filler

Quarter inch hardware cloth is the standard. NC State Extension recommends quarter inch hardware cloth over vents and does not recommend expanding foam as a stand-alone seal. Rodents chew soft materials, so metal mesh is what keeps an opening closed. (Source: NC State Extension, common structure-invading rats)

Screening vents against rats

Chicken wire and thin screen are not enough. UC IPM recommends half inch mesh over vents and says chicken wire is not enough to stop rats. NC State's quarter inch hardware cloth is the tighter standard, and it also keeps mice out. (Source: UC IPM, rats)

Screens should be fastened on all four sides, not just stapled at the top. If soil has washed away from the base of a vent, the gap below the screen is an opening of its own.

Access Doors, Pipes and Foundation Gaps

Door gaps and sweeps

The access door should close tight against the frame. NC State Extension advises keeping the clearance under a door to no more than a quarter inch and mentions kick plates and rodent-proof door sweeps for garage and storage doors that take wear. (Source: NC State Extension, common structure-invading mice)

What works to seal gaps

Concrete and metal close gaps that foam and wood cannot. UC IPM says gaps wider than a quarter inch should be closed with metal or concrete. Steel wool is a temporary plug, and it lists plastic, rubber, vinyl, expanding foam and wood as materials that do not hold against mice. (Source: UC IPM, house mouse)

For pipe and wire entries, a metal collar or hardware cloth fitted around the pipe and sealed with concrete or mortar is the usual fix.

Moisture, Insulation and Cleanup

Crawl spaces with torn vapor barrier or damp soil are more comfortable for rodents. A damaged barrier can be replaced after the animals are gone. Insulation that has been nested in should be removed and replaced following the cleanup steps.

Do not sweep or vacuum dry droppings

Dry droppings under the house should be wetted, not swept. The CDC says not to sweep or vacuum dry droppings or nests, because that puts dust into the air. Wet them with disinfectant first and wipe them up. (Source: CDC, rodent cleanup)

More From the Published Guidance

Why a dead rodent can end up in a wall

The EPA says anticoagulant baits take about 4 days to 2 weeks to kill. A poisoned rodent can die inside a wall, attic or crawl space, which is why trapping is preferred indoors. Delayed action is the main reason indoor bait causes odor problems. (Source: US EPA, rodenticides)

Cleanup steps from the CDC

The CDC advises airing out the space for 30 minutes, wearing gloves, soaking droppings and nests with disinfectant, wiping them up with paper towels, double bagging the waste and washing hands afterward. Following the order matters as much as the supplies. (Source: CDC, rodent cleanup)

Telling the two rats apart

UC IPM gives roof rats at about 5 to 10 ounces and Norway rats at about 7 to 18 ounces. The species matters because roof rats work from above and Norway rats from the ground, so trap placement differs. Size is one more clue to add to droppings and nest location. (Source: UC IPM, rats)

Storms and flooding push rodents indoors

NC State Extension notes that flooding pushes rodents into attics and that Norway rats are the most common rat in North Carolina. After a storm, new gaps and wet debris are both worth checking. A quick walk around the property after a storm is time well spent. (Source: NC State Extension, pest problems after storms: rodents)

What rat droppings tell you

NC State Extension puts a rat at roughly 30 to 50 droppings a day, each about a half to three quarters of an inch long. Fresh, dark droppings in a spot mean the route is in use now. In practice, that makes droppings the first thing to photograph and count. (Source: NC State Extension, common structure-invading rats)

Rub marks and travel routes

NC State Extension lists rub marks along walls and pipes among the signs of rat travel. Those marks follow the same route night after night, which is where inspection and trapping effort pays off. Following the marks is often faster than searching a whole attic. (Source: NC State Extension, common structure-invading rats)
