Rodent Control for Fayetteville Apartment Managers: A Multi-Unit Protocol Guide

Published March 5, 2026

In a multi-unit building, rodents move between units. This guide gives managers a plan that covers the reporting unit, its neighbors and the common areas.

Key Takeaways

  • Treating only the reporting unit leaves the building as a source
  • Seal shared wall and chase gaps with metal, not foam
  • Check common areas, trash rooms and laundry rooms

Why One Unit Is Not Enough

How fast mice multiply

A small group becomes a large one fast, and it spreads. UC IPM reports that a female house mouse can have 5 to 10 litters a year of about 5 to 6 pups, with gestation of 18 to 21 days and sexual maturity at 5 to 6 weeks. A few mice can become dozens in a season. (Source: UC IPM, house mouse)

In an apartment building, rodents use shared walls, pipe chases and ceiling voids to move between units. A treated unit with an untreated neighbor is likely to see animals again. That is why the plan covers the reporting unit, the ones above, below and beside it, and the common areas.

Step 1: Collect Reports

Ask residents where and when they saw or heard activity. Mark the units on a floor plan. Clusters on one side of a building often point to a route or entry.

Step 2: Inspect the Building

What mouse droppings tell you

Droppings help map where mice travel. NC State Extension puts a mouse at roughly 50 to 75 droppings a day, each about a quarter to a half inch long. A small area can hold a lot of them, which is why a mouse problem is often found by droppings first. (Source: NC State Extension, common structure-invading mice)

How small a gap a mouse needs

Pipe gaps behind sinks and in closets are usually larger than this. NC State Extension reports that an adult mouse can get through an opening of about a quarter inch. A gap that size is easy to miss at a door corner, a pipe collar or a vent edge. (Source: NC State Extension, common structure-invading mice)

Check under sinks, behind stoves, in water heater closets, along the base of shared walls and in the trash room. Outside, check the foundation, vents and dumpster pad.

Step 3: Trap and Seal

Where mouse traps go

Trap lines follow walls and corners. UC IPM says mice seldom travel more than about 30 feet from the nest, so traps are spaced no more than about 10 feet apart and set in pairs 1 to 2 inches apart along walls where mice run. (Source: UC IPM, house mouse)

What works to seal gaps

Metal and concrete close gaps; foam does not. UC IPM says gaps wider than a quarter inch should be closed with metal or concrete. Steel wool is a temporary plug, and it lists plastic, rubber, vinyl, expanding foam and wood as materials that do not hold against mice. (Source: UC IPM, house mouse)

Checking traps daily

Daily checks need a plan for who does them and how access is arranged. NC State Extension advises checking traps daily and keeping mouse traps for mice and rat traps for rats. A trap that is too small for the animal can injure it without killing it and teach it to avoid the spot. (Source: NC State Extension, common structure-invading mice)

Step 4: Common Areas and Trash

Laundry rooms, trash rooms and storage areas are where food waste and cardboard accumulate. Keep lids closed, clean spills and keep boxes off the floor. If dumpsters are outside, keep them on a clean pad away from the building where possible.

Step 5: Communicate with Residents

Tell residents what is happening and what they can do: store food in sealed containers, report signs early and give access when asked. Explain that the work covers the whole building. Residents are more cooperative when they understand why.

Prevention is the main control

Sanitation and sealing protect everyone in the building. The CDC states that controlling rodents in and around the home is the primary way to prevent rodent-borne disease such as hantavirus. Sealing and sanitation come before any trap or bait. (Source: CDC, rodent control)

More From the Published Guidance

Telling the two rats apart

For Rodent Control for Fayetteville Apartment Managers: A Multi-Unit Protocol Guide: UC IPM gives roof rats at about 5 to 10 ounces and Norway rats at about 7 to 18 ounces. The species matters because roof rats work from above and Norway rats from the ground, so trap placement differs. Size is one more clue to add to droppings and nest location. (Source: UC IPM, rats)

Baiting a burrow

For Rodent Control for Fayetteville Apartment Managers: A Multi-Unit Protocol Guide: The EPA allows outdoor burrow baiting only with pelleted bait placed at least 6 inches down the burrow. Bait left on top of the ground is a hazard to pets and wildlife. Burrow work is for outdoor, controlled use only. (Source: US EPA, rodenticide product restrictions)

Rub marks and travel routes

NC State Extension lists rub marks along walls and pipes among the signs of rat travel. Those marks follow the same route night after night, which is where inspection and trapping effort pays off. Following the marks is often faster than searching a whole attic. (Source: NC State Extension, common structure-invading rats)

Door gaps and sweeps

NC State Extension advises keeping the clearance under a door to no more than a quarter inch and mentions kick plates and rodent-proof door sweeps for garage and storage doors that take wear. A strip of daylight under a door is a gap that a rodent can use. (Source: NC State Extension, common structure-invading mice)

Why a dead rodent can end up in a wall

The EPA says anticoagulant baits take about 4 days to 2 weeks to kill. A poisoned rodent can die inside a wall, attic or crawl space, which is why trapping is preferred indoors. Delayed action is the main reason indoor bait causes odor problems. (Source: US EPA, rodenticides)

Screening vents against rats

UC IPM recommends half inch mesh over vents and says chicken wire is not enough to stop rats. NC State's quarter inch hardware cloth is the tighter standard, and it also keeps mice out. Mesh size is a small detail that decides whether a vent stays closed. (Source: UC IPM, rats)
